CSU in 2030: two ways this could go

The main page shows that CSU's gap comes back every year. This page asks what the university looks like in the 2030–31 year under two different responses: closing each gap as it arrives, or making large decisions early. Both are plausible. Neither is painless.

This page is different from the rest of the site

  • It is a scenario exercise, not evidence. The other pages report what public documents say. This one runs simple arithmetic forward on assumptions chosen by the author with an AI model. Change the assumptions and the results change.
  • It is not a CSU plan and not a prediction. No one at CSU has proposed either path in this form. The scenario names and descriptions are the author's.
  • It names no programs, units or people. Which things a university should stop doing is a judgment for the people who govern it.
  • Every number is labeled. Scenario result means computed from the assumptions; Our assumption means chosen by the author; the other labels mean what they mean elsewhere on the site. The full list of assumptions is at the bottom.

1

What both scenarios have to live with

The pool of students is shrinking, the costs keep rising, and the state is not coming.

Fewer 18-year-olds

The number of U.S. high school graduates is projected to have peaked in 2025 and to fall 13% by 2041. Colorado's is projected to fall 14%. Two states that send CSU many nonresident students fall much further: California 29% and Illinois 32%. Outside report News report

Nonresidents already leaving

Nonresident enrollment fell from 9,997 in fall 2024 to 8,422 in fall 2026. Second-year retention slipped from 87.9% to 86.4%. CSU document

Costs that rise on their own

In FY27, pay, benefits and mandatory costs rose $42.3M while tuition rate increases raised $24.0M. A 1% raise costs about $5.5M; a 1% increase in state funding brings about $2.1M. CSU document Our arithmetic

Both scenarios use the same starting gap, the same tuition increases, the same state funding and the same inflation. They differ in three things only: how the gap is closed, what happens to pay, and what happens to enrollment as a result. The third is the most uncertain and matters the most.

2

The two paths

One spreads the pain thinly and indefinitely. The other concentrates it early and bets on a recovery.

Year by year

Close each year's gap as it arrives

  • Every fall a new gap is announced. Each college and division gets a percentage target.
  • Units cut what can be cut fastest: vacant lines, instructors on short contracts, advisors, staff, operating budgets.
  • About a quarter of each gap is covered with one-time money, as in FY27. It comes back the next year.
  • Raises are held near 1% to limit the cuts.
  • No program is closed on purpose. Many shrink below the size at which they work.

Decide early

Choose the smaller university on purpose

  • In 2027, leadership and shared governance decide what size CSU will be and which programs it will grow, merge or end.
  • The whole FY28 gap is closed with permanent decisions. No one-time money is used to defer it.
  • A one-time transition fund ($35M assumed, from reserves) pays for severance, teach-outs and consolidation.
  • Part of the savings is reinvested in recruiting, retention and a few new programs.
  • Raises return to 3% by FY30, for a smaller workforce.

3

What 2030 looks like

The dollar totals are closer than the experience of working or studying there.

Our inference The descriptions below are the author's judgment about how each set of numbers would play out, informed by what has happened at other public universities facing similar gaps. They are not outputs of the arithmetic.

Year by year

Fall 2030

  • A fifth round of cuts is under way. The FY31 budget opened about $37M short, and FY32 will too. Every budget year since FY27 has brought a new reduction target.
  • The cuts fell where they were easiest, not where they made sense. Roughly 740 positions are gone since 2026, mostly through vacancies, retirements and non-renewals. Which departments lost people depended on who happened to leave.
  • Almost everything still exists, thinner. The catalog looks much as it did. Many programs have too few faculty to offer required courses on schedule, so sections are larger and students take longer to finish.
  • Pay has fallen about 8% behind inflation. The people with the most options leave first, and searches to replace them are frozen.
  • Enrollment keeps sliding. Nonresident students number about 6,600, down from 9,997 in 2024. Each year's cuts make the next year's recruiting harder.
  • The balance sheet absorbs what the budget cannot. Covering a quarter of each gap with one-time money means drawing on reserves, and maintenance is the usual place to defer.

What this path has going for it: no single year forces a decision that cannot be undone, no program is closed in public, and if enrollment or state funding surprises on the upside, little has been given up. It is also the path every incentive favors, because each unit's rational response to a target is to protect itself.

Decide early

Fall 2030

  • The worst year is three years behind. FY28 closed the whole $66M gap with permanent decisions: roughly 270 positions in one year, compared with about 200 under the other path. It included layoffs, and programs that many people cared about ended or merged.
  • CSU is smaller and built for the students it has. It plans around 7,600 nonresident students, not 9,997. Total positions eliminated since 2026 are roughly 640, a similar order of magnitude to the other path.
  • Fewer programs, staffed to work. What remains can offer its courses on schedule. Administrative work that every college used to do separately is done once.
  • Raises are back to 3%. Pay ends about 3% behind inflation rather than 8%, and has kept pace since FY30.
  • The yearly gap is down to about $8M. That is a size ordinary budgeting can handle, and no deferred one-time money is waiting to come due.
  • Some of it did not work. A plan this large will include consolidations that saved less than promised and new programs that did not fill.

What this path costs: a brutal first year, real harm to people who did nothing wrong, a fight over who decides, and bad headlines at exactly the moment CSU needs applicants. Program closures draw national attention and can deter the applicants a recovery depends on. It also requires leadership with the standing to carry it, at a time when the president's office is in transition.

4

Where this could be wrong

“Decide early” only comes out ahead if enrollment responds. If it does not, it is the more expensive path.

  • Enrollment is the whole question. The scenarios assume nonresident enrollment keeps falling 6% a year under one path and levels off under the other. Both are guesses. The demographic projections say the pool shrinks either way through 2041.
  • The arithmetic is simple on purpose. It carries forward FY27's cost increases and the ratio of positions to dollars from FY27's cuts. Real budgets have many more moving parts, and CSU has not published its FY28 line items.
  • Things left out. Federal research funding, a recession, the move to a new athletic conference, online enrollment, changes in state policy after the 2026 election, and the outcome of the Amendment 87 lawsuit could each move the result by tens of millions in either direction.
  • The two paths are ends of a range. A real response would mix them. The value of drawing the extremes is to see what each one trades away.
  • Who wrote the descriptions. The narrative in section 3 was drafted by an AI model under the author's direction. It reflects general patterns from other universities, not inside knowledge of CSU.

How the arithmetic works. FY28 uses CSU's projected gap and assumes total tuition is level with FY27 (rate increases roughly offsetting the enrollment loss CSU has described). For each later year, the gap equals any one-time money used the year before, plus cost growth (raises on a payroll base of $551M, benefits, mandatory costs, financial aid, new investments), minus revenue growth (tuition rates and enrollment applied to FY27 tuition of $254.1M nonresident and $227.9M resident, state funding of $211.1M, and any new revenue). Permanent cuts reduce the payroll base by 60% of their value. Positions are estimated at 4.1 per $1M of permanent cuts, the FY27 ratio. Pay after inflation compounds each year's raise against 3% inflation.
